Renting versus buying gets treated like a personality test: are you a homeowner type or a renter type? That framing skips the only question that matters for your finances, which is whether buying builds more net worth than renting and investing the difference. This is a spreadsheet decision, not a lifestyle one, and it deserves the same discipline you'd apply to any five- or six-figure financial commitment.

The math depends on your specific numbers: your local market, your interest rate, how long you'll stay, and what you'd otherwise do with the cash you're not putting into a down payment. Business owners we work with across Minnesota and Wisconsin ask us this question constantly, usually right after they've asked about retirement contributions or cash flow planning for the year. It's the same category of decision: a large, long-term allocation of cash that deserves more than a gut check.

## Renting and buying are both cash flow decisions

Every month, you're either paying a landlord or paying a mortgage lender, a county assessor, an insurance company, and eventually a roofer. Neither option is free, and neither one is inherently the smarter move. What changes the answer is how long you stay, what your money would earn if it weren't tied up in a house, and what actually happens to home values and rents in your market over that period.

The useful exercise isn't asking "should I buy a house," it's asking "what does my personal balance sheet look like in 5 years under each scenario." That means projecting equity built, investment growth on money not spent on a down payment, and total cash outflow under both paths side by side.

## What buying a home costs beyond the mortgage payment

A mortgage payment is the visible cost. It's rarely the full cost. On a median-priced home with a conventional loan, expect these additional line items:

| Cost category | Typical range | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Property taxes | 1.0%–1.5% of home value annually | Annual, often escrowed monthly |
| Homeowners insurance | $1,200–$2,500 annually | Annual, often escrowed monthly |
| Maintenance and repairs | 1%–2% of home value annually | Ongoing, irregular timing |
| Closing costs | 2%–5% of purchase price | One time, at purchase |
| PMI (if under 20% down) | 0.5%–1.5% of loan balance annually | Monthly, until 20% equity |

On a $400,000 home, that's roughly $4,000–$6,000 a year in taxes, $1,500 or so in insurance, and another $4,000–$8,000 a year that should be set aside for maintenance, even in years you don't spend it. Skip that line item and you're not comparing costs accurately, you're just deferring them to the year the furnace fails.

## What renting costs that a lease doesn't show you

Renting looks simpler because one number covers almost everything. The hidden cost is what economists call opportunity cost: the return you give up by not owning an appreciating asset, and the protection you give up against rent increases.

But renting has real financial advantages that get dismissed too quickly in casual comparisons. A renter isn't exposed to a $12,000 roof replacement or a special assessment on a shared driveway. A renter can move for a job opportunity in Madison or Minneapolis without absorbing a 6% transaction cost on the sale of a house. And a renter who takes the money that would have gone toward a down payment and invests it consistently isn't automatically behind, especially over shorter time horizons.

According to the Federal Reserve's [Survey of Consumer Finances](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf20.pdf), homeowners report substantially higher median net worth than renters, but that gap reflects decades of forced savings through mortgage principal payments and market appreciation, not a guarantee that buying wins in every 3- to 7-year window. Timing, holding period, and what happens to the money not spent all matter more than the raw statistic suggests.

## Running the numbers side by side

A reasonable comparison needs four inputs: your expected holding period, your down payment amount, a realistic estimate of home appreciation in your market, and a realistic estimate of investment returns on the alternative. Here's a simplified version using a $400,000 home, 20% down ($80,000), a 6.5% mortgage rate, and a 5-year holding period.

| | Buy | Rent and invest the difference |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront cash | $80,000 down + ~$12,000 closing costs | $0 |
| Monthly housing cost | ~$2,900 (principal, interest, taxes, insurance) | ~$2,200 rent |
| Monthly difference invested | $0 | $700/month at 7% average return |
| 5-year equity/investment value | ~$95,000 in home equity (principal paid + 3% annual appreciation) | ~$52,000 invested, plus original $92,000 if invested instead at 7% ≈ $129,000 |
| Net position after 5 years | ~$95,000 | ~$181,000 |

Change any one input, a longer holding period, a lower rate, faster local appreciation, and the outcome flips. That's the point: there's no universal answer, only your numbers. Run this with your actual rate, your actual market's appreciation history, and your actual investment discipline before you sign anything.

## Where the mortgage interest deduction helps and where it doesn't

The mortgage interest deduction gets more credit in casual conversation than it deserves in practice. Under current law, mortgage interest is deductible only if you itemize, and the standard deduction is high enough now that most homeowners don't itemize at all. According to [IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505), you can generally deduct interest on acquisition debt up to $750,000, but that deduction only has value once your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction threshold for your filing status.

For a household with a $400,000 mortgage, that deduction might shave a few thousand dollars off taxable income in the early years when interest payments are highest, less as the loan amortizes. It's a real benefit, but it's a supporting detail in the decision, not the deciding factor. Anyone buying a home primarily for the tax deduction is solving the wrong problem.

## The opportunity cost of your down payment

The money that goes into a down payment stops being liquid the moment you close. It's not available for a business opportunity, a market downturn buying opportunity, or an emergency without a home equity loan or a sale. For business owners already managing [a balanced investment portfolio](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it), a large home purchase is a portfolio decision as much as a housing decision, and it should be evaluated against your other allocations, not in isolation.

This is where the emotional framing of homeownership causes the most financial damage. A down payment that stretches a household to its limit, leaving no reserve for maintenance or a slow month in a business, creates fragility that a rent payment usually doesn't. If a $12,000 roof repair would force a client to carry a balance on a credit card, that's a signal the numbers aren't ready yet, regardless of how the market is trending.

## Turning the decision into a financial plan

The households and business owners who get the most value from this analysis treat it the way they'd treat any capital allocation decision: with a cash flow projection, a realistic maintenance reserve, and a clear-eyed comparison of what the money does in each scenario. That's the same discipline behind budgeting and expense management for a small business, and it applies just as directly to a personal balance sheet. If recurring costs already feel hard to track in your household, the habits in [managing subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep) apply just as well to home maintenance reserves and escrow surprises.

Buying a home isn't a bad decision and neither is renting. The bad decision is skipping the projection and deciding based on what a market is doing this month or what a friend just closed on.

## Common Questions

**Is renting better than buying financially?**
It depends on your holding period, local appreciation rates, and what you'd do with the money not spent on a down payment. Over short holding periods, typically under 3-4 years, renting and investing the difference often wins because transaction costs on a home purchase and sale eat into any equity gained. Over 7+ years in a stable or appreciating market, buying usually pulls ahead.

**How much should I budget for home maintenance each year?**
A common rule of thumb is 1% to 2% of the home's value annually, though older homes and homes with major systems (roof, HVAC, foundation) nearing the end of their useful life should budget toward the higher end.

**Does the mortgage interest deduction make buying worth it?**
Usually not by itself. Under [IRS Topic No. 505](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc505), the deduction only helps if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction, which excludes many households. Treat it as a modest offset to borrowing costs, not a primary reason to buy.

**What's a reasonable down payment if I also run a business?**
Enough to secure a competitive rate, typically 20% to avoid PMI, while still keeping 3-6 months of both personal and business operating expenses in reserve. If a down payment would drain your business's cash cushion, that's a sign to revisit the timeline before the timing of the market.
