Medical bills rarely show up on schedule. A torn ACL, a surprise diagnosis, a root canal that turns into a crown, and suddenly a family that was current on every bill is staring down a five-figure balance. The Federal Reserve's [Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/report-economic-well-being-us-households.htm) has tracked this for years: a meaningful share of U.S. households say they'd have to borrow money, sell something, or simply not pay a surprise expense in the $400 range. Scale that up to a hospital stay or a specialist referral, and the gap between what people have set aside and what they owe gets dangerous fast. An emergency fund helps close that gap. It doesn't close the bigger one, which is the tax treatment of how you pay for care in the first place.

Most of the advice on medical debt stops at "save three to six months of expenses." That's sound, but it's incomplete for anyone who itemizes deductions, runs a small business, or has access to a high-deductible health plan. The real defense against medical debt isn't a bigger cushion. It's using the tax code the way it's designed to be used, before the crisis hits.

## An emergency fund is the backup plan, not the strategy

Cash reserves matter because they buy time. They don't reduce what you owe. A $10,000 emergency fund covering a $9,000 ER visit and follow-up care still means $9,000 spent with after-tax dollars and zero tax benefit recovered. Compare that to the same $9,000 routed through a Health Savings Account, where the contribution was already deducted from taxable income and the withdrawal for qualified care is tax-free. Same bill, same cash outflow, but one version costs you real tax dollars and the other doesn't.

We tell clients at Slachta Accounting to think of cash savings and tax-advantaged accounts as two separate layers. The savings account is liquidity. The HSA, FSA, and itemized medical deduction are the tools that determine how much of that liquidity the IRS lets you keep. Skipping the second layer means paying full price for care you could have partially subsidized through your own tax return.

## What an HSA actually does for your tax bill

If you're enrolled in a qualifying high-deductible health plan, a Health Savings Account is the single strongest tool available for medical cost planning. It works on three fronts at once: contributions reduce your taxable income, growth inside the account isn't taxed, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses come out tax-free. No other account in the tax code offers that triple benefit.

For 2024, the contribution limits are $4,150 for self-only coverage and $8,300 for family coverage, with an extra $1,000 catch-up allowed once you turn 55. Unlike a Flexible Spending Account, HSA balances carry over indefinitely. There's no "use it or lose it" deadline, and the account stays with you if you change employers or become self-employed. For a dentist running a solo practice or a small business owner paying for a family health plan, that's a meaningful pool of tax-sheltered money sitting ready for the year a kid breaks an arm or a spouse needs surgery.

Business owners have a second angle worth considering: pairing a high-deductible plan with employer HSA contributions can be a lower-cost way to support staff benefits than a richer traditional plan, while giving employees the same tax advantages. If you're weighing plan design decisions for your team, that's a conversation worth having alongside your CPA and your benefits broker, not after open enrollment closes.

## FSAs work differently, and timing matters

A Flexible Spending Account also lets you set aside pre-tax dollars for medical costs, but the mechanics are different enough that mixing up the two causes real financial mistakes. FSA elections are capped at $3,200 for 2024, funds are generally forfeited if unused by year end (subject to a limited grace period or carryover your employer may offer), and the account belongs to your employer, not you. If you leave the job, the balance typically doesn't follow you.

| Feature | HSA | FSA |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Yours, portable | Employer's plan |
| Rollover | Unlimited, carries forever | Limited or none |
| Eligibility | Requires high-deductible health plan | Available with most employer plans |
| 2024 contribution limit | $4,150 / $8,300 (family) | $3,200 |
| Investment growth | Tax-free | Not applicable |

An FSA still has a place, especially for predictable annual costs like orthodontia, glasses, or routine prescriptions where you can estimate the spend accurately. The mistake we see most often is treating an FSA like a savings account for unpredictable emergencies. It isn't built for that. If your health plan qualifies you for an HSA instead, that account is the better home for anything tied to an unexpected medical event.

## The medical expense deduction most taxpayers miss

Beyond the accounts, the tax code has a direct deduction for medical costs that a surprising number of people never claim. Under [IRS Publication 502](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502), if you itemize deductions, you can deduct unreimbursed medical and dental expenses that exceed 7.5% of your adjusted gross income. That threshold sounds high until a real crisis hits it. A household with $120,000 in AGI clears that bar once qualified medical expenses pass $9,000, and a bad year with surgery, physical therapy, or a long hospital stay gets there quickly.

Qualified expenses under Publication 502 go well beyond hospital bills. Mileage to medical appointments, certain home modifications for a medical condition, long-term care insurance premiums up to age-based limits, and even some travel costs for treatment can count. The catch is that this only helps if you itemize, and the standard deduction has been high enough since 2018 that many households don't. Anyone facing a heavy medical year should run the math both ways before filing, because a single bad year is often exactly when itemizing pulls ahead.

## Building the plan before the bill arrives

The households and business owners who handle a medical crisis without lasting damage almost always did three things ahead of time: they had cash reserves for the first hit, they had an HSA or FSA structured correctly for their coverage, and they kept clean enough records to claim the itemized deduction if the year turned into a bad one. None of that requires guessing at the future. It requires a plan built during a calm year, not during the emergency room visit.

For business owners, this ties directly into cash flow discipline generally. If your business budget already has slack cut from [subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep) or unused software, that's capacity you can redirect toward HSA funding or a stronger emergency reserve without touching payroll or growth spending. And medical costs are only one exposure worth sizing correctly. If you haven't reviewed how much [insurance coverage](/resources/insuring-where-it-counts-avoid-over-insuring-in-the-wrong-pl-2) you actually carry against your real cash flow and net worth, a medical event is exactly the scenario that exposes a gap. Long-term illness and care costs deserve the same scrutiny; our piece on [long-term care planning](/resources/long-term-care-how-you-can-financially-prepare-for-the-expen) covers how those bills compare to acute medical events and what funding options exist.

None of this is a one-time decision. Health plan elections, HSA contribution levels, and itemizing decisions get revisited every year, ideally before the plan year locks in each fall.

## Common Questions

**How much should I keep in an emergency fund for medical bills specifically?**
Most financial advisors suggest three to six months of expenses as a general reserve, but for medical costs, a more useful benchmark is your health plan's out-of-pocket maximum. If your family plan caps out-of-pocket costs at $9,000 a year, that figure is a more precise target than a generic multiple of monthly expenses.

**Can I use HSA funds for a medical bill from a previous year?**
Yes. There's no deadline to reimburse yourself from an HSA as long as the expense was incurred after the account was opened. Keep the receipt and documentation, since the IRS can ask for proof the withdrawal matched a qualified expense.

**Do I need to itemize every year to benefit from the medical expense deduction?**
No. You can itemize in a year with heavy medical costs and take the standard deduction in other years. Compare both every filing season, since a single expensive year can make itemizing worthwhile even if the standard deduction wins in typical years.

**What happens to unused FSA funds at the end of the year?**
Under standard IRS rules, unused FSA funds are forfeited unless your employer's plan includes a grace period (typically up to 2.5 months) or allows a limited carryover. Check your plan document before year end so you're not leaving money on the table.
