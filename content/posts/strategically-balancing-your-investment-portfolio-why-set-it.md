A portfolio that felt right in 2015 doesn't look the same today. Build a 60/40 mix of stocks and bonds a decade ago and never touch it, and a strong equity market alone can push you to 75/25 or higher without a single decision on your part. That's not diversification working in your favor. That's risk quietly creeping upward while the account statements still look fine.

This is the trap behind "set it and forget it." It sounds disciplined, even wise. In practice, it means your asset allocation strategy drifts every quarter markets move, and most investors don't notice until a downturn hits a portfolio that's far more aggressive than they realized. The Federal Reserve tracks exactly this kind of shift in household investment behavior, and the pattern holds: allocations move with prices, not with a plan. You can read more in the [Federal Reserve's notes on household finance and investment behavior](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/default.htm).

## Rebalancing is a discipline, not a one-time decision

Portfolio diversification isn't something you set up once and check off a list. It's closer to maintaining a building. You don't insulate the attic once and assume the whole house stays warm forever. Markets move at different speeds. Equities can run for years while bonds sit flat, or a sector you're overweight in can quietly become half your account. Rebalancing brings the mix back to the target you actually chose, whether that's 70/30, 50/50, or something built around a specific goal like funding a buy-sell agreement or a practice sale in ten years.

For a dentist in Stillwater with a SEP-IRA and a taxable brokerage account, or an attorney with a 401(k) through the firm plus outside investments, this drift often happens in two places at once. The retirement account rebalances automatically if it's in a target-date fund. The taxable account usually doesn't, and that's the one most people ignore because selling triggers a tax bill they'd rather not think about.

## Where taxes decide how you rebalance, not just whether

This is where the accounting side of the conversation matters as much as the investment side. Selling appreciated stock to bring your allocation back in line can trigger capital gains tax, and the rate depends entirely on how long you've held the position and what tax bracket the gain lands in. A business owner having a strong year might be better off rebalancing inside a retirement account, where trades don't create a taxable event, rather than selling winners in a brokerage account and adding to an already high income year.

There are other tools worth knowing before you sell anything:

- Directing new contributions toward underweighted asset classes instead of selling overweighted ones
- Using tax-loss harvesting in down years to offset gains realized elsewhere
- Coordinating the timing of a rebalance with a lower-income year, a business sale, or a year you're already itemizing deductions

None of this replaces a financial advisor's job of picking investments. It's the layer where a CPA adds value: making sure the rebalancing your advisor recommends doesn't create a tax surprise that erases part of the gain you were trying to protect. The AICPA's [personal financial planning resources](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/topic/personal-financial-planning) cover this overlap between tax strategy and investment decisions in more depth.

## Risk tolerance isn't fixed, even if your allocation is

Risk tolerance investing gets treated like a personality trait you determine once, on a questionnaire, and never revisit. It's not. A 42-year-old insurance agency owner two years from a partner buyout has a different risk tolerance than the same person did at 32, even if their answers to a generic quiz haven't changed. An architect who just took on a construction loan for a new office has less room for portfolio volatility than one who paid off the building years ago.

Business ownership adds a layer most generic investment advice skips entirely: your business is already a concentrated, illiquid bet on one industry, one local market, one set of clients. If your personal portfolio is also heavily weighted toward stocks in that same sector, or worse, toward your own company's stock or a note tied to your practice, you're doubling down on risk you're already carrying five days a week. A balanced investment portfolio for a business owner often means leaning more conservative outside the business than a generic model would suggest, precisely because so much personal net worth is already tied up in the company.

## A rebalancing checkpoint that actually gets used

Most people don't rebalance because they don't have a trigger. "Check it sometime" never happens. A specific rule does.

| Trigger | Typical action |
|---|---|
| Any asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from target | Rebalance that account |
| Calendar date (e.g., every January, alongside tax planning) | Review full allocation across all accounts |
| Major life or business event (sale, inheritance, new debt) | Reassess risk tolerance and target mix |
| Large capital gain realized elsewhere in the year | Rebalance inside tax-advantaged accounts first |

The specific thresholds matter less than having one at all. A 5-point drift trigger and an annual calendar review, paired together, catch most of the risk creep that "someday" never does.

## Common Questions

**How often should I rebalance my investment portfolio?**
Most financial professionals suggest reviewing allocation at least annually, with a rebalance triggered any time an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from its target. Business owners with concentrated income years should also review before any major sale of appreciated assets, since the tax timing can matter as much as the trade itself.

**Does rebalancing always trigger a taxable event?**
Only in taxable brokerage accounts, and only when you sell a position with a gain. Rebalancing inside a 401(k), IRA, or SEP-IRA doesn't create a tax bill, which is why many advisors and CPAs coordinate to shift the trades into retirement accounts first whenever possible.

**What's the difference between asset allocation and diversification?**
Asset allocation is the split between broad categories like stocks, bonds, and cash. Diversification is how spread out you are within each category, across sectors, company sizes, and geographies. You can have a diversified stock portfolio that's still badly out of balance if stocks have grown to dominate your overall mix.

**Should my CPA or my financial advisor handle rebalancing?**
Your financial advisor typically executes the trades and sets the target allocation. Your CPA's role is making sure the timing and method don't create an avoidable tax cost, particularly around capital gains, retirement contributions, and business income timing.
