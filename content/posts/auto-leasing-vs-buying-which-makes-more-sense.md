A dentist in Hudson, Wisconsin calls us every March with the same question: should the practice lease the new SUV or buy it outright? The dealership finance guy has an answer. So does the accountant down the street. So does a cousin who swears by one or the other. Most of that advice ignores the two things that actually matter: your cash flow pattern and how the vehicle interacts with your tax return. Get those two right and the decision usually becomes obvious.

This isn't a post about which option is cheaper in a vacuum. Leasing and buying solve different problems. The right call depends on whether you drive 8,000 miles a year or 30,000, whether the vehicle sits on a business balance sheet or your personal one, and whether you'd rather have a predictable payment or an asset you can sell in five years.

## The real question isn't the monthly payment

Dealerships sell leases on monthly payment size because a lower number closes deals faster. That's a marketing decision, not a financial one. A $450 lease payment and a $650 loan payment on the same vehicle aren't equivalent choices, they're different financial products with different total costs, different tax treatment, and different exit options.

The better questions are: How many years do you typically keep a vehicle? Does your business use it for client meetings, job sites, or deliveries? And what does your cash flow look like month to month, not just today? A service-based business with seasonal revenue swings has different constraints than a dental practice with steady collections.

## How leasing works for your cash flow

Leasing trades ownership for predictability. You pay for the vehicle's depreciation during the lease term plus interest (called the money factor), not the full purchase price. That usually means a lower monthly payment and little or no cash tied up in a down payment.

The tradeoff shows up at the back end. Mileage caps, typically 10,000 to 15,000 miles a year, generate overage charges that run 15 to 30 cents per mile if you exceed them. Wear-and-tear charges at lease-end can surprise owners who didn't read the inspection standards closely. And when the lease ends, you have no asset. You're either signing a new lease, buying out the old vehicle, or starting the search over.

For a business owner who replaces vehicles every two to three years and wants payments that don't compete with payroll and rent for cash, leasing keeps monthly outflow predictable and frees up capital for other uses.

## How buying works for your cash flow

Buying, whether with a loan or cash, means a larger monthly payment or upfront outlay but an asset that's yours once the loan is paid off. According to the [Federal Reserve's Consumer Credit report](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/), auto loan balances remain one of the largest categories of household revolving and non-revolving credit, which tells you plenty of people are financing rather than leasing, often because they keep vehicles longer than a typical lease term allows.

Once the loan is paid off, the monthly cost disappears (aside from insurance and maintenance), and you can drive the vehicle for years with no payment at all. That's the appeal for owners who keep vehicles seven, eight, or ten years, or who put on high mileage running a service route or visiting job sites across Minnesota and Wisconsin.

The cash flow hit is heavier early. A 20 percent down payment on a $55,000 vehicle ties up $11,000 that a leasing option wouldn't require. For a business managing tight margins, that's real money pulled out of working capital.

## The tax angle that actually moves the decision

Here's where the CPA lens changes the math for business owners. If the vehicle is used for business, the tax treatment of leasing versus buying is genuinely different, not just a matter of which line item you deduct.

With a purchased business vehicle, you may be able to use the Section 179 deduction to expense a significant portion of the purchase price in the year you place it in service, subject to weight-based limits and business-use percentage rules. Heavier vehicles (generally SUVs and trucks over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight) qualify for larger first-year deductions than passenger cars, which are subject to IRS luxury auto depreciation caps. The specific rules on what qualifies, how business-use percentage is calculated, and how depreciation recapture works if you sell the vehicle early are laid out in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946), and they're detailed enough that guessing costs money.

With a leased business vehicle, you generally deduct the business-use percentage of the lease payment itself, plus operating costs like fuel, insurance, and maintenance. There's no depreciation deduction because you don't own the asset, but there's also no depreciation recapture headache if you turn the vehicle in early.

The practical effect: a dentist buying a $70,000 SUV that's used 80 percent for practice-related travel might see a substantial first-year deduction under Section 179 that a lease could never generate. An attorney who leases a sedan and uses it 40 percent for client meetings gets a smaller, steadier deduction with none of the depreciation complexity. Neither is automatically better. It depends on how much taxable income you're trying to offset this year versus spreading deductions over several years.

## Lease vs buy at a glance

| Factor | Leasing | Buying |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly cash outflow | Lower | Higher |
| Upfront cash needed | Minimal or none | Down payment, often 10-20% |
| Mileage limits | Yes, 10,000-15,000/year typical | None |
| Ownership at end | None, unless you buy out | Full ownership |
| Business tax treatment | Deduct business-use % of payment | Section 179 / depreciation on business-use % |
| Best for | Vehicles replaced every 2-3 years | Vehicles kept 5+ years or high mileage |

## Driving habits that tip the decision

If you're consistently under 12,000 miles a year, drive a newer vehicle every few years, and want to avoid maintenance costs on an aging car, leasing tends to fit. If you drive 20,000+ miles a year visiting clients, job sites, or multiple office locations, mileage overage charges alone can erase whatever monthly savings a lease offered.

Business owners in field-heavy work, contractors, home health providers, and sales-driven service businesses, tend to lean toward buying because the mileage and eventual resale or trade-in value work in their favor. Professionals with lower, more predictable mileage, think office-based attorneys or architects who occasionally drive to client sites, often find leasing's lower payment and newer-vehicle cycle a better cash flow fit.

## A quick framework for business owners

Before signing anything, walk through four questions with your accountant, not the finance manager:

1. What's the vehicle's realistic annual mileage under normal business use?
2. How many years do you actually keep vehicles before replacing them?
3. Do you need a large deduction this year, or would a steady deduction over several years serve your tax planning better?
4. What does the payment do to monthly cash flow given your business's revenue pattern, especially if you're a seasonal or project-based business?

## Common Questions

**Can I deduct 100% of a leased business vehicle's payment?**
Only if the vehicle is used 100% for business, which is rare for most owners. You deduct the business-use percentage of the lease payment, based on a mileage log or documented usage split between business and personal driving. Mixed-use vehicles require you to track and support that percentage if the IRS asks.

**Does Section 179 apply to leased vehicles?**
No. Section 179 is a purchase-based deduction tied to ownership and depreciation. Leased vehicles are deducted through the business-use percentage of the lease payment instead, as detailed in [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946).

**Is it better to buy a car through my business or personally?**
It depends on business-use percentage, your entity type, and whether you want the deduction on the business return or your personal return. A vehicle used primarily for business and titled to the business generally supports a cleaner Section 179 claim, but the right structure depends on your specific tax situation.

**What happens if I go over my lease mileage limit?**
You'll owe an overage fee, typically 15 to 30 cents per mile, billed when you return the vehicle. If you consistently drive more than 12,000-15,000 miles a year, that overage can erase the monthly savings leasing offered in the first place.

The lease-versus-buy decision touches your tax return, your cash flow, and how you'll be using the vehicle three years from now.
