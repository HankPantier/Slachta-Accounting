A long-term care event doesn't send a warning text three years ahead of time. It shows up as a fall, a diagnosis, or a spouse who can no longer manage alone, and suddenly a family is choosing a memory care facility on a Tuesday afternoon with no plan for how to pay for it. We work with business owners, dentists, and attorneys across Minnesota and Wisconsin who have built substantial retirement accounts and real estate equity, and most of them have never run the numbers on what a two-year stay in assisted living would do to that plan. This isn't a topic to solve in a crisis. It's a funding decision, and like any funding decision, it works better when it's made years in advance with real numbers instead of guesses.

## What long-term care actually costs

In Minnesota and Wisconsin, a private room in a nursing home commonly runs $90,000 to $120,000 a year, depending on the facility and location. Assisted living tends to fall in the $50,000 to $65,000 range annually, and a home health aide working 40 hours a week can add up to $50,000 or more a year once you account for overtime and holiday rates. None of these numbers include the cost of home modifications, medical equipment, or the informal caregiving that adult children often absorb by cutting back their own work hours.

The average nursing home stay is a little over two years, but a meaningful share of people need care for five years or longer, particularly with dementia-related conditions. Run the low end and the high end side by side and the swing is enormous: $180,000 versus $600,000 or more for the same category of care, depending entirely on how long someone needs it. That range is exactly why long-term care planning has to be treated as risk management, not budgeting.

## Three ways to fund the risk

Most families end up paying for long-term care through some combination of three sources: long-term care insurance, government benefits (primarily Medicaid and, for veterans, VA Aid and Attendance), and personal savings. Medicare covers very little of this. It pays for short-term skilled nursing after a hospital stay, generally capped around 100 days, and stops well short of the custodial care that makes up most long-term care needs. That gap is where planning either pays off or doesn't.

| Funding source | What it covers | Who it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term care insurance | Daily or monthly benefit for home care, assisted living, or nursing home care, usually with a benefit period and elimination period | Clients in their 50s to mid-60s who are still insurable and want to cap their exposure |
| VA Aid and Attendance | Monthly cash benefit added to a veteran's pension for those who need help with daily activities | Wartime veterans and surviving spouses who meet service and income/asset tests |
| Self-funding from savings | Retirement accounts, brokerage assets, home equity, or cash reserves earmarked for care | Clients with $2 million or more in liquid net worth who can absorb the cost without insurance |

## Long-term care insurance vs self-funding

Long-term care insurance makes the most sense for people who have significant assets to protect but not so much that a five-year, $600,000 care event is a rounding error. A typical policy purchased in your late 50s might run $2,500 to $4,000 a year in premium for a benefit that covers $150,000 to $250,000 of care costs, adjusted for inflation riders. The math works because you're transferring a large, uncertain cost onto an insurer for a smaller, known one, the same logic behind any insurance purchase. We walk through this the same way we help clients size other coverage, which is the subject of our piece on [how much insurance coverage you actually need](/resources/insuring-where-it-counts-avoid-over-insuring-in-the-wrong-pl-2).

Self-funding is the right call for a smaller group of clients, generally those with $2 million-plus in investable assets who can treat a care event as a large but survivable draw on the portfolio. Even then, self-funding works best when it's a deliberate decision, with a specific pool of assets earmarked and the withdrawal strategy coordinated with a financial advisor rather than pulled ad hoc from whatever account is easiest to liquidate. This is one of the areas where we sit down with a client's financial advisor directly, because the sequencing of withdrawals, the tax bracket impact, and the timing around required minimum distributions all interact with a care-cost decision. It also ties back to how the underlying portfolio is built to weather that kind of draw, which is why the discipline around [why a balanced portfolio isn't a set-it-and-forget-it decision](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) matters here too.

Most families land somewhere in between: a smaller insurance policy that caps the worst-case scenario, backed by a savings cushion for anything the policy doesn't cover. There's no single right answer, but there is a wrong one, which is deciding nothing and hoping Medicaid or a family member fills the gap.

## Veterans benefits many families miss

Wartime veterans and their surviving spouses may qualify for the VA's Aid and Attendance benefit, a monthly payment added on top of a standard VA pension for those who need help with daily activities like bathing, dressing, or managing medication. The benefit is meaningful, often several hundred to over a thousand dollars a month depending on the household, but it comes with service, income, and net worth requirements that trip up a lot of otherwise-eligible families. Full details on eligibility and the application process are on the VA's own [Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefits page](https://www.va.gov/pension/aid-attendance-housebound/).

We see this benefit get overlooked constantly, particularly among clients who assume their income is too high to qualify without checking the actual thresholds, or who don't realize unreimbursed medical and care expenses can be deducted when calculating countable income. If there's a veteran or a veteran's surviving spouse anywhere in the family who might need care in the next few years, it's worth confirming eligibility before assuming the benefit doesn't apply.

## Tax treatment of long-term care costs

Long-term care insurance premiums and unreimbursed long-term care expenses can qualify as deductible medical expenses, subject to the usual 7.5%-of-AGI floor for itemized medical deductions, and premium deductibility is further capped by age-based limits that adjust annually. Qualified long-term care services themselves, along with premiums for tax-qualified policies, are addressed directly in [IRS Publication 502 on medical and dental expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p502), which is the reference we use when we're building this into a client's tax projection. For business owners, there's also a separate conversation about whether a C-corporation or certain pass-through structures can deduct LTC premiums as a business expense on behalf of an owner-employee, which is worth reviewing with your entity structure in mind rather than assuming one rule fits every client.

## Building long-term care into your broader plan

The clients who handle this well aren't necessarily the wealthiest ones. They're the ones who treat long-term care as a line item in retirement planning the same way they treat a mortgage payoff date or a business succession timeline: something with a number attached, reviewed periodically, and coordinated across the people advising them. For a business owner or dentist in their late 50s, that usually means three conversations happening together instead of separately: what the practice or business is worth and when it might be sold, what the retirement portfolio can realistically absorb, and whether insurance makes sense to buy now while health still allows for affordable underwriting. We coordinate the tax and cash flow side of that conversation and loop in a client's financial advisor for the insurance and investment side, which tends to produce a plan that actually gets funded instead of one that sits in a drawer.

## Common Questions

**How much does long-term care insurance typically cost?**
Premiums vary by age, health, and benefit design, but a policy purchased in your late 50s with a moderate daily benefit and inflation protection commonly runs $2,500 to $4,000 a year. Buying earlier, while you're healthier, generally locks in a lower premium than waiting until your late 60s.

**Does Medicare pay for nursing home care?**
Medicare covers only short-term skilled nursing care following a qualifying hospital stay, typically capped around 100 days, and it does not cover the ongoing custodial care that makes up most long-term care needs. That gap is precisely why insurance, VA benefits, or savings need to be lined up separately.

**Can veterans get help paying for assisted living?**
Wartime veterans and surviving spouses who meet service, income, and net worth requirements may qualify for the VA's Aid and Attendance benefit, a monthly payment that can be applied toward home care, assisted living, or nursing home costs. Details on eligibility are on the VA's Aid and Attendance and Housebound benefits page.

**Are long-term care insurance premiums tax deductible?**
Premiums for a tax-qualified long-term care policy can count as a deductible medical expense, subject to age-based dollar limits and the 7.5%-of-AGI threshold for itemized medical deductions, as outlined in IRS Publication 502.
