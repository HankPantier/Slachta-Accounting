An architect in Wisconsin called us last year holding two life insurance policies, a disability policy she'd forgotten she was paying for, and an umbrella policy sized for a net worth she'd outgrown a decade ago. She was paying roughly $4,200 a year for coverage that didn't match her actual exposure. Meanwhile, her practice, which represented most of her net worth, carried no business interruption coverage at all. That's the pattern we see constantly: too much insurance in low-risk corners, not enough where a single event could wipe out years of savings.

Most insurance advice treats coverage like a shopping list: get life insurance, get disability, get an umbrella policy, check the boxes. That approach misses the actual question, which is a math problem, not a checklist item. How much would a specific loss cost you, and how much of that cost can your cash flow or net worth absorb without insurance? Everything above that line is what you insure. Everything below it, you're often paying to insure twice.

## Insurance is a math problem before it's a shopping list

Every insurance decision comes down to two numbers: the probability of a loss and the severity if it happens. A $30,000 loss that you could cover from savings without much disruption doesn't need a policy built around it. A $2 million loss that would end your business or your family's financial security does. The confusion happens when people insure against probability (how likely something feels) instead of severity (how much it would actually cost).

Take two business owners with identical annual revenue of $800,000. One runs a service-based consulting firm with no inventory, no real estate, and low liability exposure. The other owns a dental practice with $650,000 in equipment, a build-out lease, and patient risk exposure that never fully goes away. Both might feel the same size on a P&L, but their insurance needs analysis produces completely different numbers. Sizing coverage off revenue or gut feeling, rather than actual exposure, is how the first owner ends up over-insured and the second ends up dangerously exposed.

## Personal coverage sized against net worth, not a rule of thumb

The old formula, "buy 10 times your income in life insurance," ignores what you're actually trying to protect. The real question is: if you died tomorrow, what dollar amount would your dependents need to replace lost income and pay off debt, and how much of that gap does your current net worth already cover?

Here's how that plays out for a business owner with $900,000 in net worth, including $500,000 in home equity and a business interest, two kids, and a spouse earning $70,000 a year. If the family needs $90,000 a year in income for 15 years until the kids are independent, that's roughly $1.35 million in income replacement need. Subtract the spouse's earning capacity and liquid net worth, and the actual gap might land closer to $700,000 to $900,000 in term life coverage, not the $1.5 million a generic multiplier would suggest.

Umbrella liability works the same way. If your net worth is $1.2 million and your auto policy caps out at $300,000 in liability, you have an $900,000 hole that a single serious accident could expose. A $1 million umbrella policy, layered on top of standard auto and homeowners liability limits, usually costs $200 to $400 a year for that protection. That's cheap relative to the exposure it closes.

Disability insurance deserves the same scrutiny, especially for dentists, attorneys, and other professionals whose income depends entirely on their ability to work. A dentist earning $250,000 a year who becomes unable to practice isn't facing a temporary setback; they're facing the loss of their primary asset. Own-occupation disability coverage, not the cheaper any-occupation version, is usually the right fit here, and it's a conversation worth having alongside whoever manages your investment accounts, not in isolation.

## Business coverage tied to what would actually sink you

On the business side, the risk-based approach applies with even more force, because business losses hit cash flow directly and immediately. A useful business insurance coverage checklist starts with naming the specific exposure, not the policy type.

| Exposure | Coverage type | Who it matters most for |
|---|---|---|
| Physical damage to property or equipment | Commercial property insurance | Dentists, specialty healthcare practices with fixed equipment |
| Lawsuit from a client, patient, or third party | General liability | Service-based businesses, architects |
| Errors in professional advice or work product | Professional liability (E&O) | Attorneys, architects, insurance agencies |
| Forced closure from fire, storm, or covered event | Business interruption | Any business with a physical location |
| Data breach or ransomware event | Cyber liability | Firms handling client financial or health data |
| Loss of a key owner or partner | Key person life insurance | Firms with 1-3 owners generating most of the revenue |

The mistake we see most often isn't skipping a category entirely; it's buying the cheapest version of coverage in every category instead of the right depth in the two or three that matter most. A law firm with minimal physical inventory doesn't need aggressive property coverage, but it absolutely needs professional liability limits that match its largest historical client engagements. An architecture firm facing a lawsuit over a design defect years after project completion needs coverage that extends past the policy period, not just during it.

Business insurance premiums are generally deductible as an ordinary business expense, with specifics laid out in [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). That deductibility doesn't change the sizing decision, but it does mean the after-tax cost of adequate coverage is lower than the sticker price suggests, which shifts the math in favor of closing real gaps rather than trimming premiums at the margins.

## A needs analysis you can run in twenty minutes

A proper insurance needs analysis doesn't require an actuary. It requires four steps, done honestly:

1. List every asset and income stream exposed to loss: your practice, your home, your investment accounts, your earning capacity.
2. Estimate the maximum realistic loss for each, not the average case. What would a total loss actually cost?
3. Subtract what your cash reserves, emergency fund, or net worth could absorb without insurance.
4. Insure the remaining gap, and only that gap, with the right policy type.

Run that exercise once a year, or whenever a major change hits: a new building lease, a second location, a child born, a spouse leaving the workforce. Coverage that fit two years ago doesn't automatically fit today. Business owners we work with through [outsourced accounting](/industries/entrepreneurs-established-small-businesses) engagements tend to revisit this alongside their annual budget and KPI review, because the same cash flow numbers that drive a budget also drive an accurate needs analysis.

## Where over-insuring quietly drains cash flow

Over-insurance rarely looks dramatic. It shows up as small, recurring charges that add up: a rider on a homeowners policy covering jewelry you don't own anymore, duplicate accidental death coverage bundled into three different accounts, an extended warranty on a business vehicle that's already covered under a commercial auto policy. None of these individually breaks a budget. Together, they behave like the recurring charges we've written about in [subscription creep](/resources/take-back-control-of-subscription-creep): small, invisible, and steadily pulling cash away from goals that matter more.

Whole life insurance sold as a retirement vehicle is the biggest one we flag for business owners in their 30s and 40s. If the goal is death benefit protection, term insurance covering the actual income-replacement gap, paired with the premium difference invested separately, usually outperforms whole life on both cost and flexibility. Whole life has a place for specific estate planning or business succession situations, particularly for owners funding a buy-sell agreement, but it's frequently sold as a default rather than a fit.

On the business side, we've reviewed policies for insurance agency clients and specialty healthcare practices carrying overlapping general liability and umbrella coverage that duplicated limits already sitting in a professional liability policy. Every dollar spent on redundant coverage is a dollar not going toward retirement plan contributions, equipment upgrades, or the cash reserve that actually protects the business during a slow quarter, a point we've also raised in looking at [why small businesses go bankrupt despite steady sales](/resources/quick-reads/why-small-businesses-are-going-bankrupt-and-how-to-avoid-it).

## Common Questions

**How much life insurance coverage do I actually need?**
Calculate the income your dependents would need annually, multiply by the number of years until they're financially independent, then subtract your liquid net worth and any spouse income. The remainder is your term life coverage target, not a flat multiple of your salary.

**Do I need an umbrella policy if I already have auto and home insurance?**
If your net worth exceeds your auto and homeowners liability limits combined, yes. A $1 million umbrella policy typically runs a few hundred dollars a year and closes a gap that a single lawsuit could otherwise expose in full.

**Are business insurance premiums tax deductible?**
Generally yes, as an ordinary and necessary business expense. [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) outlines which premiums qualify and where the exceptions apply, particularly around life insurance where the business is the beneficiary.

**How often should I redo my insurance needs analysis?**
At minimum once a year, and immediately after any major change: a new lease, a second location, a child, a spouse's job change, or a significant jump in net worth. Coverage sized for last year's numbers rarely fits this year's exposure.
