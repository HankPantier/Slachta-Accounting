A cabin on a Wisconsin lake or a condo near the Gulf changes more than your weekend plans. It changes how the IRS looks at your tax return, and the difference comes down to one number: how many days a year you rent the place out versus use it yourself. Cross that line and a relaxing getaway turns into a rental business with its own reporting rules, deduction limits, and record-keeping demands. Stay under it and you keep the simpler tax treatment of a personal residence, mortgage interest deduction included.

Most of what shows up when you search for second home tax advice comes from real estate agents talking about square footage and resale value. That's useful for the purchase decision, but it skips the part that actually determines your tax bill: the IRS doesn't care what you call the property. It cares how you use it.

## The 14-day rule that decides your tax treatment

If you rent the property for 14 days or fewer during the tax year, you don't report the rental income at all, regardless of how much you collected. You can still deduct mortgage interest and property taxes as itemized deductions, the same way you would for your primary home. This is the rule that lets someone rent their lake place for the Fourth of July week or a hunting opener weekend without touching a Schedule E.

Cross 14 rental days and the income becomes reportable. From there, the IRS splits your expenses between personal use and rental use based on the number of days each applies, and how much you can deduct depends on how the property gets classified. [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527) walks through the mechanics in detail, and [IRS Tax Topic 415](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc415) covers the reporting basics for rental and vacation property.

## Personal use vs. rental use: how the days actually get counted

This is where most owners miscount without realizing it. A day counts as personal use if:

- You or a family member stays there, even if you pay fair rental price
- You let someone stay there for free or at a reduced rate
- You swap use of the home with another owner under a reciprocal arrangement

A day does not count as personal use if you're there mainly to perform repairs or maintenance, and that distinction matters more than it sounds. An owner who spends a weekend replacing a dock or repainting a bedroom can document that as maintenance time rather than personal use, which shifts the ratio in their favor.

Once personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days the home was rented at fair value, the IRS treats the property as a personal residence with rental activity attached. That triggers a specific limitation: your rental deductions can't exceed your rental income. No loss carries over to offset your W-2 or business income that year, though unused expenses generally carry forward.

## Second home vs. investment property: what changes on your return

The label you put on the property doesn't matter to the IRS. The use pattern does. Here's how the three common scenarios play out:

| Use pattern | Reporting | Deduction limits |
|---|---|---|
| Rented 14 days or fewer per year | No rental income reported | Mortgage interest and property tax deductible on Schedule A |
| Personal use exceeds 14 days or 10% of rental days | Rental income reported on Schedule E | Expenses limited to rental income; no loss allowed |
| Personal use stays under the 14-day/10% threshold | Rental income reported on Schedule E | Full expenses and depreciation allowed; losses may be deductible up to $25,000 if you actively participate, subject to income phase-outs between $100,000 and $150,000 AGI |

That third row is the one people searching "second home vs investment property" are usually trying to reach. It requires keeping personal use genuinely minimal, which for a family cabin is harder than it sounds. A long Fourth of July stay, a few weekends in the fall, and a week at Christmas can eat through 14 days before Labor Day.

## What you can deduct once rental income applies

Once the property crosses into rental territory, the expense list expands considerably: mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, utilities, cleaning and maintenance, association dues, and depreciation on the structure itself. Each of these gets allocated between personal and rental days based on total days used during the year. Days the home sat vacant generally don't count toward either side of that ratio, which surprises owners who assume an empty week adds to their personal-use column.

Depreciation is the deduction most owners underuse. A rental home's structure (not the land) gets depreciated over 27.5 years under IRS rules, and that deduction reduces taxable rental income even in years the property throws off positive cash flow. It also creates depreciation recapture exposure at sale, which is worth planning around well before you list the property.

## A lake cabin example from Wisconsin

Consider a business owner client who buys a cabin near Hayward, Wisconsin. They use it 20 days a year themselves and rent it out through a platform for 40 days at market rate. Personal use of 20 days exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of 40 rental days (4 days), so the property falls into the limited-deduction category: rental expenses can offset rental income, but any excess doesn't create a deductible loss that year.

Now change the numbers. The same client cuts personal use to 12 days and rents the cabin 60 days a year. Personal use now falls under the 14-day threshold, which opens the door to full expense deductions, depreciation, and a potential loss deduction against other income, subject to the $100,000 to $150,000 AGI phase-out for active participation. Twelve days of difference in usage changes the entire tax posture of the property.

That's the calculation worth running before you decide how often to block off the calendar for personal use, and it's a different conversation than the one a realtor or property manager will have with you.

## Common Questions

**What is the IRS 14-day rule for a second home?**
If you rent your second home for 14 days or fewer during the tax year, you don't report that rental income on your return, no matter the amount collected, and you keep the standard mortgage interest and property tax deductions available to any personal residence.

**Is renting out my vacation home considered a business?**
Not automatically. It becomes a reportable rental activity once you exceed 14 rental days, but whether losses are deductible depends on your personal-use days relative to rental days, tracked under the rules in [IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527).

**Should I buy a second home as an investment property instead of a personal one?**
That depends on how you plan to use it. Treating it as an investment property with minimal personal use (under 14 days or 10% of rental days) opens up full deductions and depreciation, but it also means genuinely limiting your own time there, which many owners find harder in practice than on paper.

**Do I need to report rental income if I only rent a few weekends a year?**
If total rental days stay at 14 or fewer for the year, no. Once you exceed 14 days, all of that rental income becomes reportable, even the portion collected during those first 14 days.
