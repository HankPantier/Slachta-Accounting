Ask ten people how much insurance they need and you'll get ten different lists: life insurance, disability, umbrella, general liability, cyber, key person. What you won't get is math. Insurance coverage isn't a shopping list, it's a risk-based calculation that starts with two numbers: what you're worth and what you'd lose if a specific bad event happened next Tuesday. Get those numbers right and the right coverage amounts fall out of the calculation. Skip them and you end up over-insured in places that don't matter and dangerously exposed in the ones that do.

We see this pattern constantly with dentists, attorneys, architects, and service-business owners across Minnesota and Wisconsin. A dental practice owner carries a disability policy sized for a solo associate's income from ten years ago, while the practice itself has no buy-sell funding if a partner becomes permanently disabled. An insurance agency owner has excellent E&O coverage but no plan for what happens to cash flow if a key producer leaves with half the book. The policies exist. They're just aimed at the wrong risks.

## The real question is what you can absorb, not what you can buy

Every insurance decision is really a decision about who absorbs a loss: you, your business, or the insurer. If you have $400,000 in liquid net worth and a lawsuit exposure that could realistically reach $2 million, that gap is what needs coverage. If your business could survive a $15,000 equipment loss out of cash reserves without blinking, insuring that risk with a low deductible is money spent on a problem you could already solve yourself.

This is the piece most insurance conversations skip. Agents sell products. A needs analysis starts from your balance sheet and asks what dollar amount of loss would actually change your financial position, then works backward to a coverage number. The IRS treats most business insurance premiums as deductible under [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535), which is useful to know, but deductibility shouldn't be the reason you buy a policy. Exposure should be.

## Start with your balance sheet, not a stack of policies

A personal risk management plan begins with three figures: net worth, annual cash flow needs, and the dollar value of what would be catastrophic to lose. For a 45-year-old architect with $900,000 in retirement accounts, a paid-down home, and two kids approaching college, the catastrophic risks are usually disability income and liability from a professional errors claim, not a large term life policy that was sized when the mortgage was bigger and the kids were toddlers.

Run the numbers instead of guessing. A rough framework:

| Risk category | Question to answer | What sizes the coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Life insurance | What income or debt obligation disappears if you die? | Outstanding debt plus years of replacement income needed, minus existing assets |
| Disability income | How long could you cover expenses with savings alone? | Monthly fixed expenses times months of reserve gap |
| Umbrella liability | What single event could exceed your auto or homeowner's limits? | Net worth exposed to a lawsuit above underlying policy limits |
| Business liability | What claim would exceed what the business could pay from operating cash? | Realistic claim size minus cash reserves and existing coverage |

Most people who feel "under-insured" have never actually run this table. They've bought whatever an agent recommended at a life event, life insurance at the mortgage closing, disability through an employer's default election, and never revisited it against current net worth. Net worth changes every year. Coverage rarely gets adjusted to match it.

## How business owners should size coverage against what the business can't survive without

For a service-based business or a specialty healthcare practice, the calculation shifts from personal net worth to operating cash flow and key-person risk. Ask three questions before renewing any commercial policy:

First, what single event would force the business to stop operating for more than 30 days? For a dental or specialty healthcare practice, that's usually a facility loss or a sudden absence of the primary provider, not a slip-and-fall claim already covered under general liability. Business interruption and key person coverage matter more here than an extra layer of umbrella limits.

Second, what claim size is realistic for your field? An architecture firm's professional liability exposure looks different from a law firm's, which looks different from an insurance agency's E&O risk. Pulling three years of industry claims data, something your liability carrier can usually provide, gives you a defensible number instead of a guess. Buying limits well beyond that realistic range is paying premium dollars for a risk that's already vanishingly small.

Third, what would happen to cash flow if a partner or key employee left tomorrow? This is where a lot of small businesses are quietly under-insured. A firm generating $1.8 million in revenue with two equal partners and no buy-sell funding is one health event away from a forced, undercapitalized buyout. That's a bigger threat to the business than most of the property coverage owners spend time optimizing. ## Where over-insuring quietly drains cash flow

Over-insurance rarely looks dramatic. It looks like a $2,000-deductible policy where a $10,000 deductible would cut the premium by 20% and the business has enough reserve to absorb the difference. It looks like a term life policy carried past the point where the mortgage is paid off and the kids are financially independent. It looks like duplicate coverage: a personal umbrella policy and a business policy both covering the same liability exposure because nobody compared the two documents side by side.

Each of these costs real money every year, money that could go toward retirement contributions, debt paydown, or reinvestment in the practice. A dentist paying $380 a month for disability coverage sized to a $220,000 income from 2015, when current income is $310,000 and savings have grown substantially, is both under-insured on the income gap and over-insured relative to what a properly structured policy would cost for the right benefit amount. The fix isn't more insurance. It's a recalculation.

## A simple exposure exercise you can run this week

Pull three documents: your most recent personal financial statement, your business balance sheet, and your current insurance declarations pages. For each policy, write down the coverage limit and then write down, honestly, the dollar loss that would actually hurt. If the limit is far above the realistic loss, you're likely over-insured there. If it's below, that's the gap to close first.

This exercise works the same way portfolio rebalancing does: risk exposure shifts as your net worth and life circumstances change, so a static coverage plan calcifies into either overpayment or a dangerous gap. The same logic behind [why a balanced investment portfolio isn't a set-it-and-forget-it decision](/resources/strategically-balancing-your-investment-portfolio-why-set-it) applies directly to insurance. Net worth grows, debt gets paid down, kids graduate, practices expand. Coverage should move with those changes, not sit frozen at whatever level made sense five years ago.

## Common Questions

**How much insurance coverage do I actually need?**
The amount depends on the gap between your net worth and your realistic loss exposure in each category: life, disability, liability, and property. There's no universal number. A useful starting point is calculating what dollar loss in each category would force a change in your lifestyle or business operations, then sizing coverage to close that specific gap rather than following a generic rule of thumb like "ten times income."

**What's the difference between personal and business insurance needs analysis?**
A personal analysis starts with net worth, dependents, and income replacement needs. A business analysis starts with operating cash flow, key-person dependency, and realistic claim sizes for your industry. Business owners usually need both run side by side, since a personal financial shock and a business one often hit at the same time.

**Is business insurance tax deductible?**
Most ordinary business insurance premiums, including liability, property, and business interruption coverage, are deductible as a business expense under [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535). Deductibility isn't a reason to buy more coverage than your exposure warrants, but it does affect the after-tax cost of the coverage you actually need.

**How often should I revisit my coverage amounts?**
At minimum, revisit coverage after any major shift: a new partner, a change in revenue, a paid-off mortgage, or a change in net worth of 20% or more in either direction. Absent a major event, an annual review alongside tax planning catches most drift before it becomes a real gap or a wasted premium.
