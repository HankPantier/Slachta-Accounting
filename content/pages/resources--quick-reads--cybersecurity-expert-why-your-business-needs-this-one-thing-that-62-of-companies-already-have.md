## The financial data risk most small businesses overlook

A recent cybersecurity report found that 62% of companies now have some form of protection in place. That number sounds reassuring until you flip it: nearly 4 in 10 businesses still have none. Owners of dental practices, law firms, insurance agencies, and service businesses tend to assume their accounting files aren't worth a hacker's time. Payroll records, tax filings, and client billing data are exactly the kind of information criminals sell or hold for ransom. Size doesn't determine the target; access does. A five-person dental office running cloud-based billing software with a bank feed connected to QuickBooks Online holds the same categories of data a breach team looks for at a much larger company, just in a smaller, often less-defended package. This isn't about fear. It's about matching your protection to what you're actually storing: Social Security numbers, routing numbers, patient billing codes, and years of tax history. That's a lot of exposure for a business that's never run a security review.

## Where accounting and payroll data is most vulnerable

Every accounting system has a few predictable weak points, and most owners never audit them until something goes wrong. Payroll files sit near the top of the list: they combine Social Security numbers, direct deposit information, and wage history in a single exportable spreadsheet, often emailed between an owner and a payroll processor. Cloud accounting logins are next. If your bookkeeper, office manager, and CPA all access the same QuickBooks Online or Xero account through one shared password, you're one weak link away from full exposure.

Bank feed connections, the automated links that pull transactions into your accounting software, are convenient but create a persistent bridge between your bank account and a third-party platform. Add in tax records stored as PDFs in a shared drive, and you've got four separate points where a single phishing email or reused password can compromise years of financial history. Recognizing these categories in your own systems, rather than treating cybersecurity as an abstract IT concern, is the first real step toward closing the gaps.

## What dental practices and professional service firms need to know

Dentists carry a layer of exposure most businesses don't: patient billing data tied to insurance claims, combining protected health information with financial account numbers in the same system. A breach touching practice management software isn't only a financial problem, it can trigger HIPAA reporting obligations on top of the usual client notification steps.

Attorneys and architects face a related version of the same risk. Client files often include settlement figures, project budgets, retainer payments, and personal financial details that opposing parties or competitors would find valuable. Insurance agencies sit on renewal data and policyholder financials for hundreds of clients at once, making a single successful phishing attempt highly efficient for an attacker. Service businesses with recurring payroll, landscaping crews, cleaning companies, contractors, face a narrower but steady risk: the same employee bank account data processed week after week, so a compromised payroll portal keeps paying out to the wrong place until someone notices. Each client type carries a different regulatory backdrop, but the fix, controlled access and verified vendors, is the same.

## Practical safeguards your CPA and IT team should have in place

You don't need a full security team to close the most common gaps. Ask your CPA firm and IT provider whether these are already in place, and if not, put them on the calendar this quarter.

- Multi-factor authentication (MFA) on every accounting, payroll, and banking login, not just the ones that require it by default
- Encrypted file sharing for tax returns and financial statements, instead of email attachments
- A secure payroll portal that lets employees update their own direct deposit information, so no one emails account numbers to a processor
- Vendor vetting for any software or bookkeeping service that touches your bank feed or client billing data
- Role-based access, so a part-time bookkeeper doesn't hold the same login permissions as your controller
- A written incident response plan, even a one-page version, naming who calls the bank, who calls clients, and who calls your CPA

That's a working list, not a compliance document. Most small businesses can put all six in place within a month without hiring outside consultants.

## Steps to take before your next audit or tax season

Before your next tax season or annual audit, run through these four items:

- Audit every password tied to accounting, payroll, and banking access, and replace any shared or reused credentials
- Review who currently has access to your accounting software and remove anyone who no longer needs it
- Confirm whether your business insurance includes cyber coverage, or whether it needs to be added
- Ask your CPA firm directly how they secure the financial data they handle for you

Twenty minutes on this list now costs far less than a breach discovered mid-filing season.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Cybersecurity expert why your business needs this one thing that companies already have

**Q: Why would a small business be a cybersecurity target?**
A: Small businesses often store the same categories of sensitive data as large companies, Social Security numbers, bank details, tax records, but with far fewer protections in place. Attackers look for access, not company size, which makes under-protected small business accounting systems an efficient target.

**Q: What accounting data is most at risk of a breach?**
A: Payroll files, cloud accounting logins, bank feed connections, and stored tax records are the most common exposure points. Each combines financial account details with personal information, and each is often protected by nothing more than a single shared password.

**Q: Do dental practices have extra cybersecurity obligations?**
A: Yes. Dental practice billing systems combine patient health information with financial data, so a breach can trigger HIPAA reporting requirements in addition to standard financial data breach notifications, making practice management software security a compliance issue, not just an IT one.

**Q: How does Slachta protect client financial data?**
A: Slachta Accounting & Consulting Services uses access-controlled platforms, encrypted payroll portals, and role-based permissions reviewed as client staff change, avoiding shared logins and email attachments for sensitive financial files across outsourced accounting and payroll engagements.
