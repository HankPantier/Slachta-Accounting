## What changed in the 2024 foreign earned income exclusion

For tax year 2024, the IRS raised the foreign earned income exclusion to $126,500, up from $120,000 in 2023. The increase comes from Revenue Procedure 2023-34, the annual inflation adjustment the agency runs on dozens of tax thresholds every fall. It sounds like a small technical update, but for a business owner running a consulting practice from Lisbon or a dentist covering telehealth shifts from abroad, an extra $6,500 of excludable income is real money. The foreign earned income exclusion 2024 adjustment matters most for people who structured their income around last year's cap and now have room to plan differently for the current filing year. It's worth revisiting your numbers before you file, not after.

## Who qualifies for the foreign earned income exclusion

The IRS doesn't require you to give up U.S. citizenship or residency to claim this exclusion. You need to meet one of two tests. The bona fide residence test looks at whether you've established a genuine home in a foreign country for an entire tax year, with intent to stay, not just visit. The physical presence test is more mechanical: you need 330 full days outside the U.S. within any 12-month period, regardless of why you're there.

That second test is the one most relevant to [entrepreneurs and small business owners](/industries/entrepreneurs-established-small-businesses) who split time between the U.S. and a foreign base, freelancers on long-term contracts abroad, and [dentists](/industries/dentists) or specialty healthcare providers running remote consultations while stationed overseas for part of the year. You don't need to be a career expat to qualify. You need to count days correctly and document them.

## How the exclusion works alongside other tax breaks

The foreign earned income exclusion doesn't operate alone, and that's where a lot of confusion starts. If you pay for housing abroad, you may also qualify for the foreign housing exclusion or deduction, which covers rent and utilities beyond a base amount, on top of the FEIE. Separately, the foreign tax credit lets you offset U.S. tax with income tax you've already paid to another country.

Here's the catch: you can't use the foreign tax credit and the FEIE on the same dollar of income. Once you exclude income under FEIE, you can't also claim a credit for foreign tax paid on that excluded portion. Many business owners assume they can stack every benefit on every dollar. In practice, you're choosing the most favorable combination for your specific income mix, and that choice can shift year to year depending on where you're taxed and at what rate.

## Reporting requirements including FBAR and foreign accounts

Claiming the exclusion doesn't remove your other filing obligations. If anything, it adds a layer most people don't expect. Foreign account reporting runs on its own set of rules, separate from your income tax return:

- Form 2555 is required to actually claim the FEIE, filed with your Form 1040
- FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) is due if your foreign accounts totaled more than $10,000 combined at any point during the year
- FATCA reporting on Form 8938 kicks in at higher account thresholds and applies to a broader range of foreign assets
- Missed FBAR filings carry civil penalties that start in the thousands, even when no tax was owed

FBAR foreign account reporting is one of the most commonly missed pieces for business owners who assume their tax preparer handles it automatically. It doesn't get filed with your 1040. It goes to FinCEN directly, and the deadline doesn't always line up with tax season the way people expect.

## Common mistakes business owners make with overseas income

### Miscounting physical presence days
icon: Clock

The 330-day test is unforgiving. Layovers, short trips home for a family event, or a miscounted travel day can knock you out of qualification for the entire year.

### Assuming residency status without documentation
icon: MapPin

Bona fide residence isn't self-declared. The IRS looks at lease agreements, local tax filings, and ties to the foreign country. Business owners who assume they qualify without paperwork often don't.

### Overlooking payroll for overseas staff
icon: Users

Entrepreneurs hiring local employees or contractors abroad run into withholding and reporting rules the FEIE doesn't touch. [Payroll services](/services/payroll-services) built for cross-border situations catch this before it becomes a penalty.

### Forgetting state tax obligations
icon: FileText

Excluding income federally doesn't mean your home state agrees. Minnesota and Wisconsin residency rules can still tax income the IRS lets you exclude, depending on how ties to the state were severed.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Irs increases foreign earned income tax breaks

**Q: What is the foreign earned income exclusion amount for 2024?**
A: For tax year 2024, the foreign earned income exclusion is $126,500 per qualifying person, up from $120,000 in 2023. The IRS adjusts this figure annually for inflation under Revenue Procedure 2023-34, so the cap typically rises each year rather than staying fixed.

**Q: Do I qualify for the FEIE if I run a business remotely from abroad?**
A: Possibly. You need to meet either the bona fide residence test, which requires an established foreign home for a full tax year, or the physical presence test, which requires 330 full days outside the U.S. within a 12-month period. Running a business remotely doesn't disqualify you if the residency test is met.

**Q: Can I claim the FEIE and the foreign tax credit on the same income?**
A: Not on the same dollars. You can exclude income under the FEIE or claim a foreign tax credit for tax paid on that income, but not both for the identical amount. Many taxpayers combine the exclusion on part of their income with the credit on the remainder for a better overall result.

**Q: Do I still need to file an FBAR if I claim the foreign earned income exclusion?**
A: Yes. The FEIE only affects income tax; it has no bearing on FBAR. If your combined foreign account balances exceeded $10,000 at any point during the year, you're required to file FinCEN Form 114 separately, regardless of whether you claimed the exclusion.
