## Accounting built around how dental practices run

A dental practice doesn't move through the year like a typical small business. Production and collections rarely land in the same month. Insurance reimbursements can trail a treatment date by 30, 60, even 90 days, which makes cash flow look worse or better than it really is if nobody is tracking the lag. Overhead ratios that look fine on a P&L can hide a hygiene program that's underperforming or a supply budget that's quietly crept up.

Slachta Accounting & Consulting Services has worked with dentists in Minnesota and Wisconsin since 2005, building financial reporting around the numbers that matter in a dental office: production per provider, collection percentage, overhead as a share of revenue, and new patient flow. That's a different starting point than a generic small business engagement built for a retail shop or a contractor. Explore how this fits your [dental practice](/industries/dentists).

## Outsourced accounting that gives you real-time financial clarity

Waiting until tax season to see your numbers means running the practice by rearview mirror. ## Payroll processing designed for dental office staffing

Dental office payroll is rarely simple. Hygienists and assistants often work varied schedules across multiple providers, pay rates can differ by role or location, and benefits deductions, PTO accrual, and bonus structures for associates add layers most generic payroll software wasn't built to handle cleanly.

[Payroll services](/services/payroll-services) from Slachta cover multi-rate processing, benefits deductions, quarterly and annual payroll tax filings, and new-hire compliance, so an office manager isn't reconciling pay discrepancies between patients. Every filing deadline gets tracked. Every pay stub reflects the actual hours and rate structure for that employee, not an approximation.

## Business tax planning and preparation for dental practices

Most dentists find out how their entity structure affects their tax bill after the fact, during filing season, when the options for that year are already gone. An S-corp election, PLLC structure, or C-corp status each carries different implications for self-employment tax, retirement contribution limits, and how income flows to the owner. Getting that structure right, and revisiting it as the practice grows, is planning work that happens well before April.

[Business tax services](/what-we-do/tax-plus) from Slachta cover entity structure review, equipment purchase timing tied to Section 179 and bonus depreciation, retirement plan contribution strategy, and quarterly estimated tax planning so there are no surprises at filing time. The difference between reactive filing and year-round planning usually shows up as real dollars: a purchase timed before year-end, a retirement contribution maximized before the deadline, an entity election filed before it's too late to use it this year.

## Retirement plans and financial planning collaboration for dentists

Retirement plan decisions for a dental practice, a SEP IRA versus a 401(k) with profit sharing, a cash balance plan layered on top, carry tax consequences that touch both the practice's books and the owner's personal return. Evaluating those options in isolation from either side misses half the picture.

Slachta works alongside the financial advisor a dentist already has, providing the practice-level financial data needed to evaluate plan design, model contribution scenarios, and confirm a plan still fits as revenue changes. That's collaboration, not a handoff. The advisor keeps the investment relationship; Slachta keeps the accounting and tax side connected to it.
