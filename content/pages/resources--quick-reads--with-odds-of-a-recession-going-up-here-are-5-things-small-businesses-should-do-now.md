## Recession odds are climbing again

Economists have been raising their recession probabilities for months, and the signals are getting harder to wave off: rate uncertainty, a cooling labor market, and forecasts that swing depending on which bank issued them. For a dentist, a service-based business owner, or a solo attorney in Minnesota or Wisconsin, none of that ambiguity matters as much as one question: is my cash position strong enough to absorb a slow quarter?

You don't need to predict the recession to prepare for it. You need visibility into your numbers now, while you still have time to act instead of react. The owners who come through a downturn in the best shape are rarely the ones who guessed right about the economy. They're the ones who already knew their break-even point, their collections trend, and their cash runway before anyone used the word "recession" in a headline.

## Tighten cash flow forecasting before you need to

Most small businesses run on historical bookkeeping: last month's numbers, reconciled after the fact. That's fine when conditions are stable. It's a liability when conditions shift fast, because by the time the report is ready, the situation has already changed.

What you need instead is a rolling 13-week cash flow forecast, updated weekly, that shows what's actually coming in and going out. Dental practices watching insurance reimbursement timing and service businesses managing uneven receivables both benefit from the same discipline: knowing your cash position two or three weeks out, not two or three months after the fact.

This is the difference between finding out you're short on payroll and seeing it coming with enough runway to adjust collections, delay a discretionary purchase, or draw on a line of credit on your terms instead of the bank's.

<!-- block: checklist-section | variant: with-image-left | image: budget-review-expenses.jpg | alt: "Business owner sorting expense categories on paper at a desk" | query: "small business owner reviewing expenses documents" -->
## Revisit your budget and expense lines

Expense management is where most small businesses either protect themselves or get caught off guard. The fix isn't cutting everything. It's knowing the difference between what keeps the doors open and what's discretionary.

Run through your last twelve months of expenses and sort each line into one of two columns before you assume you need to cut anything:

- **Essential**: payroll, rent, insurance premiums, debt service, core supplies
- **Discretionary**: non-critical software subscriptions, unbudgeted marketing spend, equipment upgrades that can wait a quarter
- **Variable but controllable**: overtime, contractor fees, inventory reorder timing
- **Recently added and unreviewed**: anything approved in the last 12 months without a follow-up check on whether it's still earning its cost

This exercise takes an afternoon and gives you a real lever to pull if revenue slows, instead of a panic decision made three months into a downturn.

## Track the KPIs that predict trouble

Annual reviews catch problems after they've already cost you money. Monthly KPI tracking catches them while there's still time to respond. Four numbers matter most heading into a slowdown.

### Production or billable output
icon: TrendingUp

For dental practices, this means production per provider per day. For service businesses, it's billable hours or completed projects per month. A dip here shows up weeks before it shows up in your bank balance.

### Collections rate
icon: Coins

The percentage of billed revenue you actually collect, tracked monthly. A widening gap between production and collections is one of the earliest signs of a client or patient base under financial pressure.

### Labor cost as a percentage of revenue
icon: Wallet

Payroll is usually the largest controllable expense on the books. If this ratio climbs while revenue holds flat, it's a signal to review staffing and scheduling before it becomes a bigger problem.

### Client or patient retention
icon: UserCheck

A drop in repeat business or recall visits often precedes a revenue drop by a full quarter. Watching retention monthly, rather than at year-end, gives you time to adjust marketing or outreach before the numbers show up in your P&L.

## Coordinate retirement and personal financial planning

A downturn doesn't just affect the business. It affects the owner's personal balance sheet, retirement plan contributions, and timeline. Owners who react to market drops by pulling back retirement contributions or making rushed personal financial moves often create a second problem on top of the first one.

This is where your CPA and your financial advisor need to be talking to each other, not working from separate assumptions. A retirement plan evaluation done in isolation from your business cash flow picture, or a personal financial decision made without checking in on how the business is actually performing, tends to compound risk instead of reducing it.

Bring your financial advisor into the conversation with your accountant before you make a move, not after.

## Frequently Asked Questions About With odds of a recession going up here are things small businesses should do now

**Q: What is the first thing a small business should do if a recession seems likely?**
A: Start with cash flow visibility. Move from monthly historical bookkeeping to a rolling 13-week forecast updated weekly, so you can see a cash shortfall coming weeks in advance instead of finding out after payroll is already due.

**Q: Which KPIs should small business owners track monthly during economic uncertainty?**
A: Production or billable output, collections rate, labor cost as a percentage of revenue, and client or patient retention. These four numbers tend to shift weeks before revenue drops show up on a profit and loss statement.

**Q: Should I cut all discretionary spending if a recession is coming?**
A: Not necessarily. Sort expenses into essential, discretionary, variable but controllable, and recently added categories first. That gives you a specific lever to pull if revenue slows, rather than an across-the-board cut that may hurt operations.

**Q: How does a recession affect my retirement planning as a business owner?**
A: A downturn can affect both business cash flow and personal retirement contributions. Coordinate decisions between your CPA and financial advisor rather than reacting alone, so retirement plan changes account for the full financial picture.
